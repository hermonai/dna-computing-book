\chapter{Generate--filter--verify algorithms}
Imagine that the last tube is empty. Have we proved that the graph has no
Hamiltonian path, or did we lose its only surviving molecule? Now imagine
a bright band in that tube. Have we found a valid path, or retained a large
off-target population? Neither observation answers the logical question until
generation, selection and readout have explicit contracts.

Chapter 11 specified strings that encode graph objects. We now separate
three levels: an exact multiset algorithm, a finite physical population,
and a decision based on observations. The reference program enumerates
small symbolic examples and evaluates assigned probability models. It does
not simulate a complete reaction network or reproduce a laboratory experiment.

\section{A population is not just a set}
\index{multiset}\index{candidate generation}
Let $\mathcal U$ be a finite universe of candidate walks. A multiset is a
function $n:\mathcal U\to\mathbb N_0$; $n(w)$ is the number of copies of
walk $w$. The support $\{w:n(w)>0\}$ contains distinct species, whereas
$\sum_w n(w)$ counts copies. Seven copies of one correct answer are one
distinct witness, not seven independent solutions to the graph problem.

Use vertices $V=\{A,B,C,D\}$ and directed edges
$A\to B,A\to C,B\to C,C\to B,B\to D,C\to D$.
Start at A and finish at D. A Hamiltonian witness visits each vertex once.
We deliberately generate walks with one through five vertex visits,
including cycles and early termination, to make the filtering task visible.
\Plate{01-pool}{The graph and representative candidates. ABCD and ACBD are
valid four-vertex witnesses; ABD omits C, while ABCBD repeats B.
The circles beside a string indicate copies, not additional vertices.
The full generated population has 34 distinct walks.}{fig:pool}
\Listing{walks}
The generator extends a frontier only along edges in $E$. Induction on
frontier length proves every emitted tuple is a directed walk. Conversely,
every walk of the next length has a prefix in the previous frontier, so
extension enumerates it. Since the maximum length is five, all possible
four-visit witnesses are covered. This is a finite digital completeness
proof, not proof that molecular assembly samples every walk.

On a general graph with maximum out-degree $\Delta$, there can be up to
$|V|\Delta^{\ell-1}$ walks with $\ell$ visits. Enumerating or physically
representing many candidates does not remove that population cost.
Our Counter holds one copy of each generated tuple; later tests deliberately
increase selected multiplicities.

\section{State the ideal filter algebra}
\index{predicate}\index{soundness}\index{completeness}
For a Boolean predicate $P$, an exact filter is
\begin{equation}
 (F_Pn)(w)=\mathbf1[P(w)]\,n(w).
\end{equation}
It neither creates candidates nor changes the multiplicity of a retained one.
Consequently $F_P^2=F_P$ and $F_PF_Q=F_QF_P=F_{P\land Q}$.
These identities follow species by species by multiplying indicator values.
\Listing{retain}

Filter first by endpoints, then by exactly four vertex visits, then by
the presence of every vertex. With exactly four positions and four required
identities, each identity occurs exactly once: if one occurred twice,
at most three distinct identities could fit. Edge validity is inherited
from the generator, not established by these filters.

The survivors are ABCD and ACBD. Here the length filter already removes all
remaining non-witnesses, so the coverage stages do not reduce the count.
That redundancy is a property of this small graph, not a license to omit
coverage on other graphs. Negative tests use malformed candidate pools so
that a filter cannot pass merely because the generator made its job trivial.

Adleman's original molecular computation combined path generation with
endpoint, length and vertex-inclusion selection~\cite{adleman}. We use that
logical pattern, not its quantities or performance, and add a separately
implemented digital certificate check.

\section{Verify a witness independently}
\index{certificate verification}
An independent verifier checks all four requirements directly:
\begin{equation}
 |w|=|V|,\quad w_0=A,\quad w_{|w|-1}=D,\quad
 \operatorname{Counter}(w)=\operatorname{Counter}(V),\quad
 (w_i,w_{i+1})\in E.
\end{equation}
The Counter equality establishes identities and multiplicities together.
The edge checks are ordered: $B\to C$ and $C\to B$ are separate facts.
\Listing{verify}
\Plate{03-logic}{Literal counterexamples separate the requirements.
The adjacency column is essential when the candidate source is untrusted.
An inclusion assay alone cannot distinguish a correct ordering from an
incorrect permutation containing the same vertex words.}{fig:logic}

For \emph{soundness}, assume the generator emits only valid walks and every
filter is exact. A survivor has the prescribed endpoints, length and coverage,
therefore is a witness. For \emph{completeness}, additionally assume every
valid witness has positive initial multiplicity. Since a witness satisfies
every predicate, its multiplicity is unchanged and it survives.

The assumptions do different jobs. Generating only walks through three visits
is sound but incomplete for our four-vertex problem. Injecting ABDC and changing
the requested endpoint to C fools endpoint/length/coverage filtering, but the
independent verifier rejects the forbidden $D\to C$ edge. A wrong generator
cannot be repaired by proving only the later filters.

Verification is inexpensive for one candidate: traverse its vertices and
edges, using suitable membership structures. Finding that candidate can
still be expensive. A failed verification is evidence about the candidate
examined, not proof that no valid candidate exists elsewhere.

\section{Physical selection implements a noisy observation}
\index{affinity capture}\index{false retention}
A complementary tethered probe can associate with a sequence-containing
strand. Association, retention during separation, and release are distinct
events. Competing strands, incomplete capture and loss during handling mean
that the physical operation is not the exact Boolean predicate.
\Plate{02-capture}{A domain-level capture mechanism. A tethered probe pairs
antiparallel with a complementary target domain; surrounding sequence stays
unpaired. The second panel distinguishes desired retained material, off-target
retention and true-target loss. Arrows represent possible outcomes, not
measured rates or an experimental protocol.}{fig:capture}

For species $w$, let $a(w)\in[0,1]$ be its probability of being retained.
If this probability is fixed and copies behave independently, then
\begin{equation}
 N'(w)\mid N(w)=n(w)\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n(w),a(w)),\qquad
 \mathbb E[N'(w)]=a(w)n(w).
\end{equation}
The expectation relation itself needs only identical marginal retention
probabilities; the binomial distribution needs independence as well.
We explicitly keep those assumptions separate.
\Listing{expected_pass}

Take ten true copies and ten thousand false copies. An assigned filter keeps
each true copy with probability $0.8$ and each false copy with probability
$0.01$. Applying it twice with unchanged independent retention gives:
\begin{center}\input{\Generated/noise-table.tex}\end{center}
After one stage, eight expected true copies coexist with one hundred false
copies. A one-percent false-retention rate is not a one-percent false fraction.
The displayed fraction is the ratio of expected counts, not generally the
expectation of the random realized fraction.

For a randomly sampled input molecule with prior true fraction $\pi$,
the probability it is true conditional on retention is
\begin{equation}
 \Pr(T\mid\mathrm{kept})=
 \frac{\pi a_T}{\pi a_T+(1-\pi)a_F}.
\end{equation}
This follows by dividing the joint probability $\pi a_T$ by total retention
probability. It exposes the base-rate dependence of a selected molecule.
Large background can dominate a selective assay.

\section{Expectation is not a survival guarantee}
\index{extinction probability}\index{detection}
Let a true molecule survive $k$ stages with conditional probabilities
$s_1,\ldots,s_k$. Its survival probability is
$s=\prod_i s_i$ when these are the appropriate sequential conditional
probabilities; no extra independence assumption between its stages is needed
for that chain rule. If stages have fixed common efficiency $0.8$ and $k=6$,
then $s=0.262144$.

Let $d$ be detection probability conditional on final survival. For $n$
initial true copies that succeed independently, each has observed-success
probability $q=sd$, and
\begin{equation}
 P_{\mathrm{detect}}=1-(1-q)^n.
\end{equation}
The complement event is that every copy fails. At $d=0.9$, the generated
values are:

\Listing{detect_probability}
The stable implementation evaluates
$-\operatorname{expm1}(n\operatorname{log1p}(-q))$ and handles $q=0,1$
explicitly. Directly subtracting $(1-q)^n$ from one loses relative precision
for tiny $q$. The test at $q=10^{-20},n=100$ checks this limit.
\Plate{04-loss}{Independent-copy detection probability for six assigned
retention stages at 0.8 followed by detection at 0.9. Twenty starting true
copies give probability about 0.9954 under this model. This is not an assay
sensitivity measurement.}{fig:loss}

To achieve failure probability at most $\epsilon$ for $0<q<1$,
take logs of $(1-q)^n\le\epsilon$:
\begin{equation}
 n\ge \frac{\log\epsilon}{\log(1-q)}.
\end{equation}
The inequality reverses on division because the denominator is negative.
Round up to an integer. Shared failures such as a failed separation step
correlate copies and can invalidate the independent-copy benefit.

Generation itself may be probabilistic. If each of $M$ independent assembly
trials creates a desired witness with probability $g$, and its conditional
survival/detection probability is $q$, then
$1-(1-gq)^M$ is the chance of observing at least one such witness.
When $g=0$, amplification of the resulting pool cannot restore the absent
sequence. When there are several witness species, their generation and
observation events require an explicitly stated joint model.

\section{When filter order changes the computation}
\index{capacity}\index{filter ordering}
Fixed diagonal retention maps commute in expectation, just like exact masks:
$a(w)b(w)n(w)=b(w)a(w)n(w)$. Different \emph{constant} efficiencies alone
do not prove order dependence. It appears when an operation changes a later
operation's inputs or admissible behavior, for example through finite capacity.

Consider ordered copy IDs [bad, good]. Filter F accepts either species but
has capacity one, using first-arrival admission. Filter G accepts only good
and has capacity two. F then G returns nothing; G then F returns good.
\Listing{capacity_filter}
\Plate{05-order}{Capacity makes filter order observable. F's only slot is
occupied by bad when it runs first. Removing bad beforehand frees the slot
for good. The deterministic admission rule is an explicit counterexample,
not a kinetic model of bead competition.}{fig:order}

Even ideal commuting filters can have different execution costs. Let
$c_F,c_G$ be cost per input molecule and $q_F,q_G$ be the fraction of the
original pool passing each filter. The expected per-original-copy costs are
$c_F+q_Fc_G$ versus $c_G+q_Gc_F$. F first is cheaper when
\begin{equation}
 c_F(1-q_G)\le c_G(1-q_F).
\end{equation}
For $c_F=1,q_F=0.1,c_G=5,q_G=0.5$, these costs are 1.5 and 5.5.
This arithmetic omits fixed setup costs, changing chemistry and capacity;
those can reverse the preferred ordering. Logical equivalence, expected
recovery and practical cost require separate arguments.

\section{Turn the final tube into a defensible decision}
A useful report distinguishes a verified positive, a negative observation
with quantified detection limits, and an inconclusive result. A verified
decoded witness establishes existence if decoding and the independent
certificate check are trustworthy. An empty tube establishes nonexistence
only under a completeness argument that covers generation, survival and
observation. Otherwise it is a negative observation under stated conditions.

Controls should test the links separately: a known true witness for recovery,
an off-target population for false retention, a blank for contamination,
and an orthogonal identity readout for the surviving material. Repeated
measurements of the same unresolved length band do not independently verify
vertex ordering. This connects the Chapter 10 observation model to the
Chapter 11 encoding contract.

Reproduce the tables with \texttt{python3 drafts/ch12/build.py --assets-only}
and run \texttt{tests/test\_ch12\_reference.py}. The tests include a
permutation-based witness oracle, wrong-generator and truncated-generator
failures, copy preservation, capacity order, and tiny-probability arithmetic.
Later formal models must preserve these distinctions rather than hide finite
populations behind an ideal set operation.

\section{Exercises with worked reasoning}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Copies.} Seven ABCD and two ABD copies enter exact filtering.
 \emph{Solution:} seven copies of one witness species remain.
\item \textbf{Coverage proof.} Why do four required identities in four positions
 imply uniqueness? \emph{Solution:} a repeat leaves too few positions for the rest.
\item \textbf{Generator failure.} What does a three-visit bound miss?
 \emph{Solution:} every four-visit witness, including ABCD.
\item \textbf{Edge check.} ABDC contains every vertex. Is it a valid A-to-C
 witness? \emph{Solution:} no; $D\to C$ is absent.
\item \textbf{Idempotence.} Prove $F_P^2=F_P$.
 \emph{Solution:} $\mathbf1[P]^2=\mathbf1[P]$ for each species.
\item \textbf{False fraction.} Compute the first noisy stage's ratio.
 \emph{Solution:} $8/(8+100)=0.074074$ true, despite one-percent false retention.
\item \textbf{Two stages.} Interpret the count 6.4.
 \emph{Solution:} an expectation over repeated trials, not a fractional molecule.
\item \textbf{Reliability.} Why are two starting copies not certain detection?
 \emph{Solution:} both can fail; $1-(1-0.2359296)^2\approx0.416196$.
\item \textbf{Required copies.} Obtain 99\% modeled detection.
 \emph{Solution:} round up $\log(.01)/\log(1-.2359296)$ to 18.
\item \textbf{Correlation.} A common failure destroys the tube with probability
 0.1. Can arbitrarily many copies guarantee 99\% detection?
 \emph{Solution:} no; success is at most 0.9 under that failure model.
\item \textbf{Order.} Trace F then G and G then F.
 \emph{Solution:} [bad] then []; [good] then [good].
\item \textbf{Evidence design.} Defend a negative conclusion.
 \emph{Rubric:} specify candidate coverage, calibrated losses and readout,
 shared failures, positive/negative controls, uncertainty, and exactly which
 nonexistence claim the evidence can or cannot support.
\end{enumerate}
\section*{Selective glossary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Selective glossary}
\textbf{Support:} species with positive multiplicity.
\textbf{Soundness:} every accepted candidate is valid.
\textbf{Completeness:} every valid candidate required by the contract is retained.
\textbf{Witness:} a candidate satisfying the full problem specification.
\textbf{Negative observation:} absence of detected evidence, not automatically
evidence of logical impossibility.
